\begin{abstract}
Dynamic community detection is commonly addressed either by recomputing a
partition on every graph snapshot or by using a solver-specific update rule.
Full recomputation preserves the behavior of mature static solvers but
repeatedly processes unchanged structure, whereas specialized dynamic methods
are less transferable across objectives, features, and implementations. This
article presents ComNetX, a hierarchical adaptation layer that replaces a
full-snapshot backend call with a sequence of contracted local calls. For each
batch, ComNetX expands the changed edge endpoints, closes this neighborhood
over the complete pre-update communities it touches, and contracts the
maintained labels before each backend call. Closure determines which community
memberships may change; contraction makes that broader update scope
computationally viable. The adapter localizes backend work but changes the
backend input and is not an objective-preserving transformation. We evaluate
ComNetX on six
batched real-graph benchmarks, 500-update runs on two larger graphs, and
five-seed dynamic stochastic block model stress streams. On the largest short
stream, local Leiden reduces cumulative measured processing time by
\ArxivShortSpeedup$\times$, while final modularity changes from
\ArxivShortFullQ\ to \ArxivShortLocalQ. Over 500 updates, the corresponding
values are \ArxivLongSpeedup$\times$ and \ArxivLongFullQ/\ArxivLongLocalQ.
At the higher synthetic update rate, random and hub-centered streams fall below
time break-even. The experiments thus identify both a useful operating region
and the conditions under which full recomputation remains preferable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{D}{ynamic} networks represent systems whose interactions change over
time, from mobile communication and online social groups to information and
scientific collaboration networks \cite{Onnela2007,Palla2007,Rossetti2018}.
Community detection summarizes such a network as groups of vertices with
coherent structural or functional roles and supports exploratory analysis,
monitoring, recommendation, and anomaly investigation
\cite{Porter2009,Fortunato2010,Radicchi2004,Flake2002}. The exact meaning of a
community depends on the chosen objective and on whether structure, attributes,
or temporal consistency are available to the method \cite{Fortunato2016,
Chunaev2020}. Modularity is one of the most widely used structural criteria
\cite{Newman2004,Brandes2008}; Louvain and Leiden are strong practical
optimizers \cite{Blondel2008,Traag2019}, and parallel implementations improve
their static throughput \cite{Lu2015,Sahu2024FastLeiden}. Their direct use on
an evolving graph nevertheless entails another solver call on the entire
snapshot after every update batch.

There are two established responses. Full-snapshot recomputation retains a
trusted static implementation and can initialize it with the previous
partition, but its workload remains tied to the full graph. Incremental and
dynamic algorithms instead revise selected parts of the previous solution
\cite{Halappanavar2017,Zhuang2021DynaMo,Zarayeneh2021DeltaScreening,
Sahu2025Dynamic}. These methods can be substantially faster, although their
localization and refinement rules are generally coupled to a particular
objective, optimizer, or data structure. This coupling matters increasingly
for attributed and representation-learning approaches, whose backend calls
may also include feature construction or iterative training
\cite{Tsitsulin2023DMoN,Liu2024MAGI,Lin2025S2CAG}. Reimplementing a distinct
dynamic procedure for every such backend is costly and makes comparisons
difficult.

The central design question is therefore how much of a changed graph should be
shown to a reusable backend. A small radius around updated edges is inexpensive
but may intersect only part of an existing community. Closing that radius over
the current partition supplies the complete membership of each touched
community, yet the resulting vertex set may be large. ComNetX addresses this
tension by contracting the maintained labels before invoking the backend. It
stores multiple label levels, expands the endpoints of each batch, freezes the
communities touched at every level, and builds compact backend instances whose
labels are projected to the original vertices.

The evaluated adapter localizes the expensive backend calls while retaining a
complete label vector for the graph. It should be interpreted as a local
approximation: contraction changes the backend instance, and the current
preprocessing still scans global sparse or label structures. The method section
states these operational boundaries precisely so that the measured
quality--time trade-off is interpreted at the correct interface.

The article makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item We introduce an affected-community closure and contraction mechanism for
batched updates. It broadens a small graph neighborhood to the complete
memberships of touched communities, then exposes compact instances through a
common partition-producing backend interface.
\item We formalize the maintained multilevel state and the implemented update
sequence, including mask freezing, contraction, projection, and successive
cuts. This specification makes the adaptation reproducible without assuming
that it preserves a backend objective.
\item We decompose workload into expanded vertices, closed community members,
and contracted backend graphs, while also accounting for the global scans in
the current implementation. The analysis explains why hub degree,
touched-community size, and contracted size govern different failure modes.
\item We map the resulting quality--time envelope on six batched streams,
500-update trajectories, mechanism and parameter controls, and five-seed DSBM
stress streams. DF-Leiden and fixed-budget S$^2$CAG calls test the reuse
boundary without being presented as universal backend rankings.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the article reviews related methods, defines the measured
problem, specifies ComNetX, describes the evaluation, presents the results, and
closes with limitations and reproducibility information.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Static Objectives and Scalable Optimization}

Structural community definitions include dense-subgraph, cut-based,
information-flow, and null-model views \cite{Porter2009,Fortunato2010,
Radicchi2004}. Modularity compares within-community edge weight with a
degree-preserving null model \cite{Newman2004,Brandes2008}. Louvain combines
local moves with graph aggregation \cite{Blondel2008}; Leiden adds a refinement
stage that addresses poorly connected communities \cite{Traag2019}. Shared-
memory heuristics and fast Leiden variants extend this family to large static
graphs \cite{Lu2015,Sahu2024FastLeiden}. Other scalable perspectives include
the map-equation formulation of Infomap \cite{Rosvall2007}, fast label
propagation \cite{Traag2023LabelPropagation}, greedy modularity disassembly
\cite{Rustamaji2024}, and iterated local moves \cite{Taibi2025FLMIG}.

No single score fully determines whether a partition is useful. Modularity has
resolution effects and a highly degenerate optimization landscape
\cite{Good2010,Fortunato2016}; comparative studies also show that algorithm
rankings depend on the planted structure and evaluation design
\cite{Yang2016Comparative}. Class labels or other metadata may describe a
different organizing principle from graph structure
\cite{Yang2015GroundTruth,Peel2017}. We therefore report structural quality and
agreement with reference labels separately, using NMI only as an external
agreement measure \cite{Danon2005}.

\subsection{Dynamic, Temporal, and Local Community Detection}

Dynamic community detection spans evolutionary objectives, incremental
partition maintenance, and continuous-time models \cite{Rossetti2018}.
Snapshot-oriented methods include incremental batch processing
\cite{Chong2013}, real-time and modularity-based label propagation
\cite{Shang2014Realtime,Ma2020IncrementalLP}, C-Blondel
\cite{Seifikar2020}, LabelRankT \cite{Xie2013LabelRankT}, and adaptive label
propagation \cite{Han2017AdaptiveLP}. DynaMo incrementally maximizes
modularity \cite{Zhuang2021DynaMo}, whereas Delta-Screening narrows the set of
vertices whose modularity decisions may change
\cite{Zarayeneh2021DeltaScreening}. NOME explicitly balances snapshot quality
and temporal smoothness \cite{Cai2023NOME}. These approaches motivate local
reuse but remain tied to their own state and update rules.

Scalable dynamic implementations include Grappolo
\cite{Halappanavar2017}, DF-Louvain \cite{Sahu2024DFLouvain}, multicore dynamic
algorithms \cite{Sahu2025Dynamic}, and distributed DyG-DPCD
\cite{Sattar2025DyGDPCD}; broader algorithmic and parallel perspectives are
surveyed by Sattar et al. \cite{Sattar2023} and Hanauer et al.
\cite{Hanauer2022DynamicGraphs}. Flow stability, Matthew-effect models, and
modularity-based tracking treat temporal persistence directly
\cite{Bovet2022FlowStability,Sun2022Matthew,Mazza2023}. Continuous-time
L-modularity avoids choosing snapshot boundaries \cite{Brabant2025LModularity},
while recent work also addresses sparse temporal observations and random-walk
snapshot clustering \cite{Conrad2025Sparse,Blaskovic2025}. ComNetX instead
targets batched snapshot maintenance; its experiments do not evaluate a
continuous-time objective.

The term \emph{local} also covers a different output contract. Seed-, query-,
and anchor-based methods find one or several nearby communities rather than a
partition of every vertex \cite{DiTursi2017,Christopoulos2023,
Liu2021MultiLocal}. OLCPM maintains overlapping communities online
\cite{Boudebza2018OLCPM}. ComNetX preserves a complete label vector and holds
labels outside each frozen mask fixed, so these methods are conceptually
related but not direct baselines.

\subsection{Attributed and Representation-Learning Methods}

Attributed community detection combines topology with node information
\cite{Chunaev2020}. The graph-neural-network model established a general
message-passing foundation \cite{Scarselli2009}; later community-oriented
methods include SEAL \cite{Zhang2020SEAL}, DMoN
\cite{Tsitsulin2023DMoN}, CommDGI \cite{Zhang2020CommDGI}, MAGI
\cite{Liu2024MAGI}, and DGCluster \cite{Bhowmick2024DGCluster}. Comparative
and survey work documents the range of embeddings, clustering losses, and
evaluation choices \cite{Kumar2024GNNStudy,Liu2026DeepGraphSurvey}. DAG learns
clusters without a prescribed cluster count \cite{Liu2024DAG}; structural-
entropy objectives provide another unsupervised signal
\cite{Zhang2025StructuralEntropy}; and fairness-aware formulations introduce
constraints beyond aggregate clustering quality \cite{Gkartzios2025Fair}.

Efficiency-oriented designs include pre-train-and-refine community detection
and inductive graph partitioning \cite{Qin2024PretrainRefine,Qin2024PRGPT},
while S$^2$CAG uses spectral subspace clustering for attributed graphs
\cite{Lin2025S2CAG}. Community structure can also be part of a downstream
dynamic prediction model \cite{Ji2023DynamicPopularity}. MFC combines matrix
factorization with a topological regularizer to learn persistent communities
\cite{Kong2024MFC}. These models do not expose identical optimization
contracts, and contraction or feature averaging changes their inputs in
different ways. Accordingly, our fixed-budget S$^2$CAG runs demonstrate that
the interface can be exercised; they do not establish backend-independent
quality preservation.

\subsection{Position of ComNetX}

Solver-specific dynamic methods localize moves, refinement steps, or state
transitions inside a particular optimizer. LD-Leiden, for example, repairs a
Leiden partition locally and exploits shared-memory parallelism
\cite{Bokov2026LDLeiden}; Sahu's dynamic Leiden starting point develops a
separate Leiden-specific update path \cite{Sahu2024Start}. Seed- and
anchor-local methods instead change the output contract by returning selected
communities. ComNetX occupies a different layer: it localizes the input given
to a partition-producing backend through affected-community closure and
contraction while retaining a label for every graph vertex. This layer is
complementary to specialized dynamic solvers. An implementation such as
DF-Leiden can be invoked at the boundary, although the evaluated local path
instantiates it anew instead of preserving its streaming state. The experiments
include DF-Leiden but no protocol-matched
LD-Leiden run, so no empirical ranking between the two is claimed.

\section{Problem Formulation and Scope}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Batched Graph Model}

Let $G_t=(V,E_t,\bm{A}_t,\bm{X})$ be the graph after batch $t$, with a fixed
vertex set $V$, $n=|V|$, sparse adjacency $\bm{A}_t$, and optional node-feature
matrix $\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$. A sparse signed batch $\bm{\Delta}_t$
updates the stored adjacency as
\begin{equation}
\bm{A}_t=\bm{A}_{t-1}+\bm{\Delta}_t.
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
Its directly affected vertices are
\begin{equation}
S_t=\{i:\exists j,\ \Delta_t(i,j)\ne0\ \text{or}\
\Delta_t(j,i)\ne0\}.
\label{eq:affected}
\end{equation}
The implementation accepts signed sparse batches. The archive identifies no
deletion experiment, but sign-level provenance is incomplete for some streams
derived from static graphs. We therefore make no empirical claim about
deletions. A changing vertex universe and evolving attributes are also outside
the measured scope.

We treat a community-detection backend as a partition-producing procedure
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}(\bm{A},\bm{X},\bm{y}_0;\bm{\theta})\longmapsto\bm{y},
\label{eq:backend}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{X}$ and the initial partition $\bm{y}_0$ are optional, and
$\bm{\theta}$ denotes backend-specific settings such as resolution or a fixed
iteration budget. The output contains one hard label per input vertex. The
current projection path additionally requires those labels to be compact
integers in $\{0,\ldots,k-1\}$ for a $k$-vertex backend instance because they
index the stored group identifiers; all evaluated adapters satisfy this
contract. A backend with arbitrary label values would require an explicit
compaction step before projection. The interface covers modularity heuristics,
attributed clustering methods, and implementations originating as native
dynamic methods when they can be invoked on a supplied instance. It does not
imply persistent backend state across calls, or that these backends optimize
the same objective or respond identically to contraction.

Full-snapshot adaptation at update $t$ invokes $\mathcal{B}$ on $\bm{A}_t$.
When the backend accepts an initialization, the previous partition is supplied:
\begin{equation}
\bm{y}^{\mathrm{full}}_t=
\mathcal{B}(\bm{A}_t,\bm{X},\bm{y}_{t-1};\bm{\theta}).
\label{eq:full_backend}
\end{equation}
The measured full Leiden baseline follows this warm-started definition. The
ComNetX state instead comprises the resident sparse adjacency, optional full
feature matrix, and $L$ full-length label vectors. Its current local call path
passes an empty warm start, even though \eqref{eq:backend} permits one. This
distinction is part of the method specification rather than a tunable choice in
the reported experiments.

\subsection{Evaluation Quantities}

All main comparisons use an undirected graph representation. Sources marked as
directed are symmetrized within the paired full/local experiment, and a
separate control retains direction. For a weighted adjacency, let
$W=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}$,
$d_i^{\mathrm{out}}=\sum_jA_{ij}$, and
$d_j^{\mathrm{in}}=\sum_iA_{ij}$. The recorded directed modularity at
resolution $\gamma$ is
\begin{equation}
Q_\gamma(C)=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{i,j}
\left(A_{ij}-\gamma\frac{d_i^{\mathrm{out}}d_j^{\mathrm{in}}}{W}\right)
\mathbf{1}\{C(i)=C(j)\},
\label{eq:modularity}
\end{equation}
which reduces to the standard undirected expression when $\bm{A}$ is symmetric,
$W=2m$, and $d_i^{\mathrm{out}}=d_i^{\mathrm{in}}=d_i$. The main experiments
use $\gamma=1$ unless a resolution sweep is stated. We also use normalized
mutual information (NMI),
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{NMI}(C,Z)=\frac{2I(C;Z)}{H(C)+H(Z)},
\label{eq:nmi}
\end{equation}
between partition $C$ and reference class labels $Z$
\cite{Danon2005}. These labels are metadata, not asserted structural ground
truth \cite{Peel2017}.

Except for the mechanism profile in Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}, the recorded
processing time is the sum of the measured optimizer/backend
intervals over the update batches. It excludes initial partition construction,
application of the sparse update to the resident adjacency, metric evaluation,
data loading, and backend-format conversion. It should therefore be read as
paired algorithm time under the repository's accounting convention, not as
end-to-end stream-ingestion latency. Speedup is the full or native-dynamic time
divided by ComNetX time under the same convention.

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} uses a distinct profile clock. It sums the separately
measured adjacency-update time and the wall interval from neighborhood
expansion through projection and cutting; backend-format conversion remains
inside that wall interval. Profile times are compared only among the three
mechanism variants within each dataset.

\section{ComNetX Framework}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Initial Hierarchy}

ComNetX stores $L$ label vectors
$\bm{C}_t=(C_t^0,\ldots,C_t^{L-1})$, with level 0 fine and level $L-1$
coarse. The bootstrap policy is fixed in the present implementation: Leiden is
run on the initial snapshot to obtain $C_0^0$, irrespective of the backend
used for subsequent measured updates. To construct level $\ell>0$, the code
contracts the current working adjacency by $C_0^{\ell-1}$, runs Leiden on the
quotient graph, restores the quotient labels to the original vertices, and
removes edges crossing the restored labels before constructing the next level.
Consequently, the initial levels are nested from fine to coarse, but the
nesting is non-strict: adjacent levels may contain the same partition. Cutting
inter-community edges before the next quotient can leave only within-group or
self-loop structure, in which case another bootstrap call need not produce a
coarser partition. The depth $L$ therefore specifies the number of stored
states and sequential backend passes, not a guaranteed number of distinct
resolutions. During an update, later passes operate after the cuts made by
earlier passes and can evolve differently even when two bootstrap levels
coincide.

This policy provides a common initial state, but it also affects interpretation.
In particular, the S$^2$CAG adapter uses an input label vector only to choose
the requested cluster count. Full-snapshot calls take that count from the
previous partition, whereas label-free local calls request one cluster per
contracted vertex. A local fixed-budget result may therefore retain much of the
Leiden bootstrap, whereas a full-snapshot feature method recomputes all labels. The
feature-backend results in Section~\ref{sec:backend_results} are therefore
interface/runtime observations rather than controlled tests of quality
preservation.

The hierarchy serves two operational purposes. At a given level it identifies
whole pre-update communities touched by the expanded batch, and its current
labels define the supernodes of the contracted backend instance. The stored
levels should not be read as multiple independently optimized outputs. Only
$C_t^0$ is evaluated and returned as the maintained fine partition; the higher
levels organize subsequent local calls.

\subsection{Frozen Affected Masks}

After applying a batch, the code expands $S_t$ by $r$ hops on the support of
$\bm{A}_t+\bm{A}_t^{\mathsf T}$. Let
\begin{equation}
B_t=N_r(S_t)=\{v:\operatorname{dist}(v,S_t)\le r\}.
\label{eq:radius}
\end{equation}
Before changing any label, it freezes one mask per level from the pre-update
state:
\begin{equation}
U_t^\ell=\{v:C_{t-1}^\ell(v)\in
C_{t-1}^\ell(B_t)\},\qquad 0\le\ell<L.
\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
Thus, $U_t^\ell$ contains every vertex whose level-$\ell$ label occurs among
the expanded vertices. Labels outside $U_t^\ell$ are not overwritten at that
level. The masks are not recomputed after an earlier backend call.

The implementation then writes vertex indices as provisional labels at level
$L-1$ for vertices in $B_t$. Because vertex indices do not occupy a reserved
label namespace, this write must not be interpreted as a guaranteed singleton
partition. For $\ell=L-2,\ldots,0$, labels from level $\ell+1$ are copied into
level $\ell$ within $U_t^\ell$. Backend calls are subsequently processed in
the opposite direction, from level 0 through level $L-1$.

\subsection{Contracted Backend Calls}

The structural working adjacency is created once by retaining only entries of
$\bm{A}_t$ whose two endpoints lie in $U_t^0$:
\begin{equation}
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{work}}_0=\bm{A}_t[U_t^0,U_t^0].
\label{eq:u0adj}
\end{equation}
At level $\ell$, let the distinct current labels among $U_t^\ell$ define
$k_\ell$ groups. A sparse membership matrix
$\bm{P}_\ell\in\{0,1\}^{k_\ell\times n}$ has one nonzero per vertex in
$U_t^\ell$. The backend adjacency is
\begin{equation}
\bar{\bm{A}}_\ell=\bm{P}_\ell
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{work}}_\ell\bm{P}_\ell^{\mathsf T}.
\label{eq:aggregate}
\end{equation}
Because \eqref{eq:u0adj} is performed only once, a vertex in
$U_t^\ell\setminus U_t^0$ can participate in the level grouping but has no
structural edge in $\bm{A}^{\mathrm{work}}_\ell$. Equation~\eqref{eq:aggregate}
is therefore not the induced adjacency of the larger $U_t^\ell$ mask.

When a backend requires features, the recorded local configuration uses mean
aggregation. For group $a$,
\begin{equation}
\bar{\bm{x}}_{\ell a}=
\frac{1}{|G_{\ell a}|}\sum_{i\in G_{\ell a}}\bm{x}_i.
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
Both contraction and feature averaging can change the effective backend
objective and input distribution. The backend returns labels for the
contracted nodes without a local warm start. These labels are projected through
the membership map, using the previous group identifiers as the label
namespace. The code then zeroes every working edge whose endpoints are outside
the current mask or have different updated labels, and proceeds to level
$\ell+1$. The reported partition is $C_t^0$.

The closure in \eqref{eq:closure} therefore supplies the membership of every
touched pre-update community, but it does not restore full-snapshot structural
context. Only edges with both endpoints in $U_t^0$ enter
\eqref{eq:aggregate}; edges from that mask to the unchanged graph are absent.
At coarser levels, closure can add vertices to the membership matrix without
adding their original adjacency. This distinction explains why closure and
contraction are evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The cut operation constrains the edges available to later calls but does not
prove that $C_t^{\ell+1}$ is a coarsening of $C_t^\ell$. No post-update nesting
assertion or repair is present. We therefore call $\bm{C}_t$ a multilevel
operational state after the first update, rather than a guaranteed dendrogram.

\subsection{Update Sequence}

For clarity, one implemented batch update consists of the following ordered
operations.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Apply $\bm{\Delta}_t$ to the resident sparse adjacency, collect $S_t$,
and expand it to $B_t$ on the support of
$\bm{A}_t+\bm{A}_t^{\mathsf T}$.
\item Before any label write, compute all $U_t^\ell$ from
$\bm{C}_{t-1}$. These masks remain fixed for the entire batch.
\item Write vertex indices as provisional labels on $B_t$ at level $L-1$ and
copy labels downward inside the frozen masks, from level $L-1$ toward level 0.
This write attempts to release affected vertices for subsequent calls, but the
indices do not constitute a guaranteed singleton namespace.
\item Restrict the working adjacency once to $U_t^0$. Then, for
$\ell=0,\ldots,L-1$, form $\bm{P}_\ell$, contract adjacency and optional
features, invoke $\mathcal{B}$ without a local warm start, project the returned
labels to $U_t^\ell$, and cut edges inconsistent with the updated level.
\item Return $C_t^0$. At every level the labels outside the corresponding
frozen mask remain unchanged; no final pass repairs or verifies nesting.
\end{enumerate}

The opposite directions in steps 3 and 4 are intentional descriptions of the
code: labels are prepared from coarse to fine, while backend calls run from
fine to coarse. This sequencing, together with the one-time restriction to
$U_t^0$, is shown explicitly in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\subsection{Cost and Locality}

Let $m_t=\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A}_t)$, $h_\ell=|U_t^\ell|$, and let
$m_0=\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A}_t[U_t^0,U_t^0])$. The current neighborhood
routine performs up to $r$ sparse matrix--vector products with the full
symmetrized adjacency, the closure routine scans $n$ labels at every level,
and the COO restriction scans the full edge-index array. Level preparation,
aggregation, and cuts then traverse label arrays and the restricted working
adjacency. Let $K_{\rm set}(n,h_\ell)$ denote the library-dependent work of the
\texttt{unique}/\texttt{isin} kernels used at level $\ell$. A transparent
implementation-level accounting, excluding backend calls and optional feature
work, is therefore
\begin{equation}
O\!\left((r+1)m_t+Ln+Lm_0+
\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\left[h_\ell+K_{\rm set}(n,h_\ell)\right]\right).
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
Mean feature aggregation adds
$O(d\sum_\ell h_\ell)$, and the backend costs add
$\sum_\ell F_{\mathcal B}(k_\ell,\bar m_\ell,d)$ for contracted edge counts
$\bar m_\ell$. The expression does not assume that set kernels are linear and
deliberately includes global scans that a backend-instance analysis alone would
miss.

The instance-dependent quantities make the source of locality more explicit.
For $r=1$, if $d_t(v)$ is degree in the symmetrized support graph, then
\begin{equation}
|B_t|\le |S_t|+\sum_{v\in S_t}d_t(v).
\label{eq:r1_bound}
\end{equation}
Thus, a small edge batch can still have a large radius-one expansion when it
touches hubs. If $\mathcal{P}_{t-1}^\ell$ is the pre-update level-$\ell$
partition, the closure size is exactly
\begin{equation}
h_\ell=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{P}_{t-1}^\ell:\,C\cap B_t\ne\varnothing}|C|.
\label{eq:closure_size}
\end{equation}
It is governed by the sizes of the communities touched by $B_t$, whereas the
backend cost is governed by the number of contracted groups and their edges.
A small neighborhood, a large closed mask, and a small contracted instance can
therefore occur simultaneously, as they do in the larger measured graphs.
Equations \eqref{eq:r1_bound} and \eqref{eq:closure_size} describe workload
geometry; they do not remove the global scans already included in
\eqref{eq:cost}.

The resident memory also includes the full sparse adjacency, $L$ full label
vectors, and the full feature matrix when used. ComNetX reduces backend-instance
size; it does not provide memory proportional only to the changed region. In
the worst case, a high-degree endpoint or a large touched community makes a
closed mask comparable with $V$, and preprocessing plus backend overhead can
erase the time advantage.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:experiments}

The evaluation addresses four questions: (RQ1) how Leiden quality and measured
processing time compare with full-snapshot warm-started recomputation; (RQ2)
how radius, hierarchy depth, closure, and contraction affect the backend
workload; (RQ3) how quality and time evolve over 500 updates; and (RQ4) where
random, hub-centered, and community-internal synthetic updates cross time
break-even. A secondary comparison checks whether DF-Leiden and fixed-budget
S$^2$CAG calls can be made through the same interface.

A real-graph strategy $p{:}q$ partitions the stored sequence into $p+1$ coarse bins,

\subsection{Methods and Fixed Configuration}

Leiden is the primary backend and uses resolution 1. Full recomputation receives
the previous partition as a warm start. ComNetX uses $L=3$, $r=1$, and the
Leiden bootstrap described in Section~\ref{sec:method}. The DF-Leiden baseline
retains one native streaming object across batches; ComNetX instead creates a
fresh DF-Leiden instance for every contracted local call. Each S$^2$CAG
invocation uses the stated feature mode and executes five internal runs with
$T=10$ each. Its full-snapshot call obtains the requested cluster count from the
previous labels, while the label-free local call defaults to the number of
contracted vertices. The S$^2$CAG rows therefore do not constitute a tuned
comparison: full and local calls see different graph sizes and cluster-count
rules, and the common Leiden bootstrap benefits the retained local state. Only
complete, protocol-matched records for
Leiden, DF-Leiden, and S$^2$CAG are analyzed. DMoN, MAGI, MFC, FLMIG, and
PRGPT are discussed as related backends but are not ranked from incomplete or
heterogeneous records. Paired rows are matched by dataset, update protocol,
and graph representation; the measurements are not used to support portable
hardware comparisons.

The main Leiden, long-horizon, resolution, and cut-metric comparisons use
paired undirected representations. A separate direction-preserving Leiden
control is reported for dyn\_pubmed and arxivmath. In the S$^2$CAG feature
ablation, dyn\_cora retains its stored direction and dyn\_pubmed is
symmetrized; each full/local pair still uses the same representation. This
control is deliberately narrower than a general validation of directed
community detection.

The mechanism profile, cut metrics, direction control, resolution sweep, and
feature ablation are separately executed supporting campaigns. Each table
compares only the full/local rows matched within that campaign. Small numerical
differences between nominally similar configurations in different tables are
therefore run-to-run differences, not alternative values for one execution.

\subsection{Result Traceability}

Tables and figures are regenerated from four source bundles containing 446

identifiers and unrounded values, verifies paired settings and series lengths,
and emits the LaTeX rows and vector figures. This traceability layer guards
against mixing directions, feature modes, or update protocols; it does not
replace the source and platform metadata discussed in

\section{Limitations and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Method Limitations}

ComNetX is a local approximation at the backend boundary. Edges crossing
$U_t^0$ are omitted, coarser affected masks do not add structural edges, and
feature averaging changes attribute distributions. The local backend receives
no warm start. Initial levels are nested, but updated levels are neither
repaired nor checked for nesting. The provisional vertex-index labels are not
guaranteed unique relative to the existing label namespace. These properties
rule out a semantics-preservation or post-update hierarchy guarantee.

The implementation retains the full graph, features, and label arrays in
memory. Neighborhood expansion uses full sparse matrix--vector products;
closure scans all labels per level; and COO filtering scans full edge indices.
Only the backend instances are consistently reduced. The main experiment clock
excludes update application, conversion, loading, and metric computation; the
mechanism-profile clock includes update application and conversion. Neither is
an end-to-end ingestion measurement, so production gains may differ.

The archived JSON records do not retain hardware identifiers, software
versions, run timestamps, or explicit repeat identifiers. The timing results
are therefore paired comparisons within the recorded runs, not portable
hardware benchmarks; exact platform provenance must be captured in any new
measurement campaign.

No adaptive local/full policy is implemented. The DSBM break-even results and
closed/contracted workload fractions identify possible policy inputs, but a
valid policy requires thresholds selected on pilot data and tested on separate
windows, together with a drift or periodic-refresh safeguard. We do not present
such a policy as an existing component.

\subsection{Empirical Validity}

All measured graphs use a fixed vertex set. The archive identifies no deletion
condition, but sign-level source provenance is incomplete for Cora, ACM, and
Citeseer; deletion performance is therefore untested. Four real-data streams
use artificial or otherwise unverified edge orders derived from static
attributed graphs and cannot support claims about natural temporal evolution.
Only patent and arxivmath use provided timestamps. The long-horizon experiment
covers one window per graph;
it has per-batch modularity and time but no per-batch NMI or partition-agreement
series. The five repeated real runs hold the stream fixed and therefore measure
runtime repeatability. Generalization across independent real windows,
deletions, evolving features, and other hardware remains untested.

The topology grid consists mostly of one run per configuration, so it is an
exploratory ablation. Each S$^2$CAG invocation executes five internal runs with
$T=10$, and full and local calls use different cluster-count rules while sharing
a Leiden bootstrap; without matched optimization budgets, multiple training
seeds, and independent cluster-count selection, it cannot rank full and local
feature-aware clustering. Reference class labels are reported as metadata,
not structural truth.

The real-graph suite ranges from 2,708 to 270,013 vertices and from 4,552 to
799,745 stored edges; the DSBM suite fixes $n=100{,}000$. It does not provide a
controlled graph-size scaling experiment. The 500-update records retain
per-batch modularity and backend time but not a corresponding long-horizon
series of affected, closed, and contracted instance sizes. Consequently, the
study identifies an operating envelope across update patterns and horizons,
but does not fit a predictive scaling law.

\subsection{Artifact Availability}

Source code, configuration files, and the archived measurement bundle are
available in the public article branch at
\url{https://github.com/mpailab/comnetx/tree/article}. The source package
accompanying this manuscript contains the exact validation program, selected
record identifiers, generated LaTeX rows, and vector figures used here.
It reproduces the reported selections and checks their settings, update counts,
and paired seeds against the archived records. The dataset sources are

continue to apply.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This article studied a practical middle ground between repeatedly running a
trusted community-detection solver on an entire snapshot and implementing a
new dynamic algorithm for every backend. ComNetX maintains a multilevel label
state, identifies the communities touched by a batch, contracts their current
labels, and invokes a partition-producing backend on compact instances. The
evaluated boundary admits topology-only Leiden, feature-aware S$^2$CAG, and
DF-Leiden calls, while the formal description distinguishes the retained
ComNetX labels from backend state that is not reused locally and explains how
labels are projected back to the full graph.

The experiments show that this design can offer a substantial quality--time
trade-off when affected communities admit strong contraction. Across six short
streams, ComNetX Leiden is faster in every paired row; on arxivmath the measured
processing-time ratio is \ArxivShortSpeedup$\times$, with modularity changing
from \ArxivShortFullQ\ to \ArxivShortLocalQ. Over 500 updates, the ratio remains
\ArxivLongSpeedup$\times$, with a larger but explicitly measured quality gap.
Closure and contraction play complementary roles: closure determines which
community members may change, whereas contraction produces the small backend
instances responsible for the largest reductions. Resolution, direction, and
feature controls show where the result is stable and where it depends on graph
or backend configuration.

ComNetX is not intended to replace specialized dynamic solvers. The current
implementation omits boundary edges from local calls, performs global
preprocessing scans, and does not enforce post-update hierarchy nesting. The
DSBM study further shows that high-rate random and hub-centered changes can
eliminate the time advantage. A natural next step is an adaptive local/full
policy trained on independent pilot windows, combined with periodic drift
checks, deletion and evolving-feature streams, controlled scaling experiments,
and end-to-end timing with complete platform provenance.
